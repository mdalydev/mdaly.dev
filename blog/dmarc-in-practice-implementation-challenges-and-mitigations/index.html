DMARC in Practice, Implementation Challenges and Mitigations

2026/11/07

Introduction

Email is one of the most widely used tools in both business and governmental communication. Given this reality, protecting the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of this channel is important not only to business operations but also to national security. DMARC is an essential part of improving email security. This essay will describe a brief history of email authentication, define important pieces of the DMARC standard, identify and offer solutions to the challenges of DMARC implementation, and discuss what DMARC doesn’t solve.

History

From its inception, email has had security limitations. Email was initially designed for use within the ARPANET environment. Functionality was the primary purpose within this environment, using assumed trust. However, as Simple Mail Transfer Protocol (SMTP) was formalized, it inherited this assumption, meaning email as we knew it was insecure. As time passed, different protocols emerged including SPF and DKIM, both aimed at better protecting email. However, there were still issues due to a lack of standard visibility and enforcement. In 2010, DMARC was created to solve these issues, which it largely has. Although the standard has been around for well over a decade, it continues to see iterations in order to better protect domains. Finally, its perceived importance and adoption have been growing. Examples of this growth include: Yahoo and Google requiring DMARC for high-volume senders, BOD 18-01 requiring all federal civilian branch agencies to implement DMARC, as well as mention in compliance frameworks such as PCI-DSS v4.0.

Defining Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting and Conformance (DMARC)

With this history outlined, it is important to explain crucial details regarding both email and DMARC.

Email, for the purposes of this paper, relies on headers for the essential function of SMTP; this is similar to the headers of a letter. When discussing DMARC, there are three particularly important identifiers: the visible From header, the SMTP envelope sender commonly reflected in the Return-Path header, and the DKIM signing domain identified in the DKIM-Signature header. The DKIM-signature is straightforward in its role of housing DKIM information. However, the ‘from’ and return-path are more easily confused. The ‘from’ header is who the message is displayed as coming from. This is generally the first piece of information recipients see, making it particularly important; it is also DMARC’s reference point. The SMTP envelope sender identifies the address to which delivery failure notifications are sent.

Whether DMARC passes or fails depends on the results from SPF and/or DKIM. Sender Policy Framework (SPF) authenticates whether a sending IP address is authorized by the domain identified in the SMTP envelope sender. DomainKeys Identified Mail (DKIM), created in 2007, evaluates against the DKIM-Signature header. DKIM leverages public and private key cryptography to sign an email, enabling both sender authentication and message integrity. Generally speaking, DKIM is often more resilient to forwarding than SPF, provided the message's signed content and headers remain intact.

Although a pass from either is required for a DMARC pass, it is not the full picture. Alignment is the other piece; it describes whether a DKIM or SPF pass applies to the domain in the ‘from’ header. For example, if the return-path is bounce@example.com but the From header is user@outlook.com, SPF cannot satisfy DMARC's alignment requirement, even if SPF authentication itself passes.

DMARC reports are crucial for their ability to provide information on who is sending email from a domain as well as each email’s authentication results. These reports are sent from receiving mail servers primarily while evaluating DMARC. With the use of XML format the reports are uniform enough to be interpreted regardless of who sends them.

The final piece of DMARC is its policies and what enforces them. The policies include: p=reject, p=quarantine and p=none. All of these instruct the receiving mail server how to handle email that fails to authenticate. First, ‘p=none’ requests no special handling based on DMARC failure. This state is intended to allow observation without impeding any mail flow. A policy of ‘p=quarantine’ specifies that if a message fails authentication, it should be flagged as suspicious; suspicious mail is then delivered to the recipient’s junk folder. Finally, a policy of 'p=reject' requests that receiving servers reject messages failing DMARC authentication, typically during the SMTP transaction. It is important to note that the exact handling of these instructions is the choice of the mail receiver.

DMARC Implementation in Theory

Now with a foundational understanding of DMARC, a conceptual process of protecting a domain can be explored. Implementation begins with a DNS TXT record displaying a policy of p=none and designating an email address for reports. From there, the domain owner collects reports to map who sends email from the domain. Given a proper dataset and the ability to analyze the data, the next step is authentication of all authorized senders. After this point policy advancement is made in steps, finishing with a policy of ‘p=reject’.

Challenges and Mitigations

Although the process appears straightforward, it is not without challenges. These challenges are present in various stages of implementation and represent a risk to achieving a policy of ‘p=reject’ as well as maintaining mail delivery. This section will discuss those challenges as well as steps to mitigate them.

The initial barrier to entry is as always, awareness. Although information on DMARC is publicly available, decisionmakers may be unaware of its existence. Overcoming this obstacle typically involves some outside influence whether that be feedback while achieving security attestation, prerequisites for cybersecurity insurance, or vendor compliance when working with a new partner. This is not exclusive to enterprises, as smaller governmental organizations often have similar blind spots. In both of these cases organizations can best overcome lack of awareness by following the Global Cyber Alliance (GCA) as well as guidelines from the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA). Both listed resources offer information on email security as well as best practices.

Once decisionmakers are aware of DMARC’s importance and a record is in place, interpreting the large numbers of XML reports is another challenge. DMARC aggregate reports summarize authentication results for messages received from a domain over a reporting period, commonly 24 hours. A single report may contain numerous records representing thousands of messages. Understanding the results of a single email is certainly possible, but it is functionally impossible for higher quantities, regardless of technical ability. A common solution is investment in one of the many DMARC aggregation services that set out to solve this challenge. These solutions work by ingesting reports and presenting the data in an understandable format.

Once the data has been presented clearly, interpreting it and applying the necessary expertise become the next challenges. Organizations may lack a dedicated IT resource that has the time and knowledge to interpret the data. This data is notoriously noisy, with reports presenting forwarders, abuse, authorized senders, as well as other noise as data without obvious distinction. Interpretation alone is insufficient, however, as configuration changes must still be made. Organizations have two primary options for addressing these challenges. First, expertise can be built internally by researching the many articles and guides available publicly. This will enable a member of the organization to both interpret and configure authorized senders. Second, professional services can often be purchased alongside many of the aggregation platforms. Both can address these challenges; however, the decision depends on a few key factors: availability of internal IT resources, budget, implementation timeline, and vendor risk policy.

Once an organization’s senders are DMARC compliant, policy adjustment finalizes this process. Confidence is the final challenge that impedes full protection of a domain. Many teams prioritize a service “working” over complete security, choosing to accept some impersonation risk over the possibility of blocking authorized traffic. With sufficient preparation, expertise, and ongoing monitoring, organizations can significantly reduce the risk of disrupting legitimate email while advancing toward enforcement. Proper mitigations include: non-working hours cutover, email delivery checks, and usage of a sufficient dataset in decision-making (~2 - 4 weeks without seasonal sending).

All of these challenges present friction in protecting a domain. However, using the mitigation techniques described above, each can be addressed and overcome.

Beyond Implementation

Once a domain has reached DMARC enforcement, there is a key question remaining, “If a member of the public, a customer, or a business partner receives mail from a trusted domain, how can they be exploited if this domain has reached p=reject?”. There are three main ways: lookalike domains, business email compromise, and display name spoofing.

Lookalike domain attacks, including typosquatting, occur when a malicious actor decides their best path of attack is to purchase a similar domain to the one they are attempting to impersonate. The purpose of this tactic is to convince a user that they are receiving mail from a trusted domain. Part of a well-rounded email security posture includes the purchasing of some similar domains as well as domains with differing top-level domains (TLD). It is also important to scan for lookalike domains as well as blocking certain inbound mail from suspicious domains.

Smaller organizations may fail to implement multi-factor authentication (MFA), leaving users with weak or reused passwords particularly vulnerable to Business Email Compromise (BEC). Implementing phishing-resistant MFA can further reduce this risk by using authentication methods tied to legitimate services, preventing attackers from reusing credentials on fraudulent sites. Additionally, account monitoring is essential for identifying suspicious activity, such as impossible travel, unusual login attempts, or unauthorized privilege changes. When attackers use a compromised account to send messages through an organization's legitimate mail infrastructure, those messages can pass DMARC despite being malicious. It is best to adopt a holistic security approach for these situations, investing in intrusion detection as well as Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR) tools.

Although there are more facets to properly protecting both an organization and its email, the final post-DMARC threat explored in this paper is display-name spoofing. The attack is a malicious use of the “Friendly” name on an email. For example, "Jamie Dimon - CEO” . In this case an email is sent from [attacker-domain.com](http://attacker-domain.com) but uses the friendly name of a recognized CEO. Mitigations primarily include user training and strong inbound email security policies that help identify and block these messages.

Conclusion

Ultimately, effective DMARC implementation requires more than publishing a DNS record. It demands visibility into an organization's sending infrastructure, careful management of legitimate email sources, and the confidence to enforce authentication policies without compromising business operations. Even after enforcement, DMARC remains one component of a broader email security strategy rather than a complete solution to impersonation and phishing.
